Tartarus

Tartarus is the primordial personified void, unique in that it functions simultaneously as an anthropomorphic Protogenos god and as a foundational realm of the cosmos.

It is not merely a "deep cavern," but a bottomless chasm that ruptured within the lowest depths of the Earth. Tartarus stands as the direct subterranean counterpart to the Sky, representing the lowest limit of the physical universe.

The Vertical Axis of the Cosmos and the Physics of the Abyss

In the archaic cosmography of Hesiod, the universe exhibits strict spatial symmetry:
Sky $\rightarrow$ Earth $\rightarrow$ Hades $\rightarrow$ Tartarus $\rightarrow$ Chaos

The scale of this vertical dimension is framed by the famous metaphor of the "nine-day anvil":

  • A bronze anvil dropped from Heaven falls for nine days and nine nights, reaching the Earth on the tenth;
  • An anvil dropped from Earth falls toward the floor of Tartarus for the exact same duration — nine days and nine nights.
Architecture of the Cosmic Penitentiary

Tartarus serves as the primary maximum-security isolation ward of creation. It is encircled by a triple belt of impenetrable Night and a bronze wall forged by Poseidon. Its solitary gates are guarded by the Hecatoncheires (Hundred-Handed Ones). Because elder gods are immortal and cannot be destroyed physically, Tartarus remains the sole mechanism for neutralizing deposed sovereign deities.

The Offspring of Tartarus

Tartarus operates not merely as an abyss-prison. When Gaia is driven to fury by Zeus confining her Titan children within the depths, she unites with murky Tartarus to produce Typhon — the most dreadful monster and a defining figure of Greek mythology.

Typhon's iconography is grotesque: a hundred dragon heads erupting with the roars of lions, the baying of hounds, and serpent hisses; flashing fire instead of eyes; colossal arms spanning from east to west. An entity so terrifying that the Olympian gods fled in panic to Egypt, disguising themselves in animal forms.

The ultimate abyss yields ultimate terror. The chthonic form of Typhon exposes the inner nature of Tartarus: the depths of the earth bring forth horrors whose very essence traces directly back to primeval Chaos.

Typhon as the Archetype of the Monster

A compelling conceptual paradigm: in subsequent ages, every manifestation of Chaos emerges as disorderly, grotesque, or deformed to varying degrees.
Typhon, however, stands as their absolute, unadulterated archetype.

Distinguishing Tartarus from Hades

In archaic thought, Hades and Tartarus designate fundamentally distinct strata of the underworld:

  • Hades is the common realm of the dead, where shades of mortal humans drift after dissolution. It is a space of mist, fading memories, and rest.
  • Tartarus is a cosmic dungeon situated immeasurably deeper than Hades. It was conceived not for ordinary dead souls, but for defeated immortal beings (Titans, Cyclopes) and paramount transgressors who defied the sacred cosmic laws of the gods (Sisyphus, Tantalus, Ixion).

The conflation of these domains emerged much later in Roman poetry (such as Virgil), where Tartarus was reduced to designating the punitive "hellish" quarter of the underworld.

Campe: The Ancient Warden of the Abyss

Before Zeus stationed the Hundred-Handed Ones at the gates of Tartarus, the abyss was governed by a monstrous warden — the dragoness Campe (Kampē), appointed by Cronus to watch over the chained Cyclopes.

Campe represented an appalling chthonic hybrid: a woman's torso terminating in a scaly dragon body, a scorpion's tail, and a hundred viper heads writhing at her feet. To enlist the Cyclopes and Hundred-Handed Ones during the Titanomachy, Zeus descended into Tartarus, engaged Campe in mortal combat, and slew the first jailer in cosmic history.

The Five Great Inmates of Tartarus

Within the deepest terraces of Tartarus, eternal sentences are served by mortals and demigods who committed boundary-shattering transgressions against cosmic law:

  1. Sisyphus: for attempting to cheat Death itself (Thanatos) and fleeing the Underworld — condemned to roll a colossal boulder to a mountain summit, which slips from his grasp at the precipice.
  2. Tantalus: for questioning the omniscience of the gods and attempting to feed them the flesh of his butchered son — stands chin-deep in water beneath boughs of ripe fruit, yet the water recedes when he stoops to drink, and branches lift away when he reaches to eat.
  3. Ixion: for an attempt to seduce the goddess Hera — bound eternally to a spinning wheel of fire.
  4. The Danaids: forty-nine sisters who murdered their husbands on their wedding night — tasked forever with filling a bottomless perforated vessel ("The Danaid Basin").
  5. Tityos: a giant who attempted to violate the Titaness Leto — stretched across the rock, where two vultures perpetually feast upon his regenerating liver.

Tartarus as the Pulsing Heart of the Cosmos (Plato, "Phaedo")

In the dialogue "Phaedo", Plato outlines a natural philosophical model of the Underworld. Here, Tartarus is not merely an inert dungeon, but a vast hollow shaft traversing the core of the Earth.

Tartarus operates like a gigantic pulsating heart of the cosmos:

  • All subterranean waters are cyclically drawn into the chasm of Tartarus and surged back out;
  • From this subterranean oscillation issue the sacred underground rivers: the river of woe Acheron, the river of fire Phlegethon, and the frozen stream Cocytus;
  • The subterranean waters swing up and down, sustaining the inner circulatory rhythm and respiration of the planet.

Pontus

Pontus (Greek Pontos — "sea") is the primordial, turbulent, salt-water sea emerging at the dawn of creation. In the cosmogony of Hesiod, Pontus is one of the initial Protogenoi, the animated watery flesh of Earth itself, concealing the abyss of the world.

Parthenogenesis of Gaia

Gaia produces Pontus parthenogenetically, without male seed, prior to her union with Uranus. If Uranus represents celestial Height, Pontus represents the fluid flesh of Earth that drained into the deepest basins of the young cosmos.

Pontus and Oceanus

Greek mythology recognizes two great primordial water deities whose identities are distinct:

  • The Titan Oceanus: the freshwater river encircling the landmass along the outer perimeter of the world. Born of Sky and Earth, he possesses personality and fathered the river gods.
  • Pontus (Protogenos): the internal salt sea, turbulent and perilous to mariners. He is elder to the Titans, representing untamed elemental water.
The Legacy of Mother Earth

The underlying connection between Pontus and Oceanus is rooted in their lineage: both trace their origin to Gaia. Mother Earth, as the source of all matter, endowed them with their fluid nature, making their waters extensions of her own substance.

Pontus as the Progenitor of Marine Monsters

Coupling with Gaia, Pontus fathered the initial inhabitants of the ocean depths, each embodying an aspect of the marine environment:

  1. Nereus: "The Old Man of the Sea", a wise and truthful prophetic divinity, father of the fifty Nereids. He personifies the calm, transparent, and benign sea;
  2. Thaumas: divinity of oceanic wonders and dangerous omens, father of Iris (the Rainbow) and the predatory Harpies;
  3. Phorcys: grim guardian of the deep, personifying submerged reefs, shoals, and hidden nautical hazards;
  4. Ceto: goddess of the marine abyss, mother of sea monsters, personifying the perils of navigation;
  5. Eurybia: "The Wide-Strong", goddess of the power of naval storms and subterranean currents.
The Origin of Chthonic Horrors

The earliest sea deities were ancient sages and monsters.
To ancient Hellenes, the salt sea was an alien element concealing perils beneath its surface. Phorcys and Ceto thus become the source of many Greek monsters: from them were born Medusa the Gorgon, the Graeae, Echidna, Scylla, and the dragon Ladon.

The Telchines: Sorcerers and Smiths of Rhodes

In addition to these five sea divinities, Pontus in union with the sea goddess Thalassa fathered the Telchines — an enigmatic race of marine sorcerers, metallurgists, and demigods native to the island of Rhodes.

The Telchines are described as therianthropic hybrids: human torsos with canine heads and webbed flippers for hands. They wielded elemental magic: summoning rain, hail, and snow, changing shape, and blighting life with their gaze (the evil eye).

The Telchines were remembered as the first metallurgists who melted bronze and iron and fashioned the earliest statues of the gods. Their zoomorphic appearance links them to the archaic motif of the Cynocephali (Dog-headed men).

Variant Tradition: The Alternative Rhodian Myth

In late Hellenistic poetry and history (Bacchylides, Callimachus, and Diodorus Siculus), the Telchines were credited with rearing young Poseidon, forging his trident, and crafting the Adamantine Sickle.

Info
  1. Bacchylides (5th century BCE): celebrated Greek lyric poet and rival of Pindar, who incorporated local island traditions into his epinician odes.
  2. Callimachus (3rd century BCE): scholar-poet and cataloger of the Library of Alexandria, who preserved obscure local variants in his verse.
  3. Diodorus Siculus (1st century BCE): author of the 40-volume "Bibliotheca Historica", who traveled the Mediterranean and recorded the traditions of Rhodes (including the Telchines in Book V).

Ultimate Role

Over time, Pontus receded from mythic narrative, dissolving into the element he represented. Dominion over the seas passed first to his son Nereus, and subsequently to the Olympian Poseidon.

Primordial Pontus remained the physical sea beneath the hulls of Greek ships. His name endured in regional geography — such as the Pontus Euxinus ("The Hospitable Sea"), known today as the Black Sea.

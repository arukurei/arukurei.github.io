Cythera (Greek Κύθηρα, Latin Cythera, Phoenician name — Porphyrousa) is a rocky island at the crossroads of the Ionian, Aegean, and Cretan seas, situated south of the Peloponnesian coast of Laconia.

In sacred geography, Cythera serves as the gateway of the Hellenic world, the initial landfall of foam-born Aphrodite, and the site of her oldest sanctuary in Hellas.

The Purple Island

Before becoming a pan-Hellenic sanctuary, Cythera was a critical harbor for Phoenician navigators traversing the sea routes from the Levant to mainland Greece.

The island’s earliest recorded name was Porphyrousa (Πορφυроῦσα) — "The Purple-Bearing Island":

The Secret of Phoenician Purple

The coastal shelves of Cythera supported dense colonies of predatory marine gastropods — murex snails (Murex brandaris).

The Phoenicians established processing installations here to extract dye from the hypobranchial glands of these snails, manufacturing Tyrian purple — a dye ranging from crimson to deep violet. Garments dyed with it were precious and served as emblems of royal and priestly authority.

Alongside this industry, the Phoenicians introduced the cult of their supreme goddess — Astarte (Ishtar), mistress of fertility, carnal passion, and navigation. Blending with native Greek religious forms, Semitic Astarte evolved on Cythera into Olympian Aphrodite.

The Ancient Sanctuary: Temple of Aphrodite Urania

Herodotus in Book I of his "Histories", investigating the provenance of cults, identifies the eastern origins of the Cytheran shrine:

The temple of Aphrodite on Cythera was founded by Phoenicians who came from the city of Ascalon in Syria. It is the most ancient of all the sanctuaries dedicated to this goddess in Hellas.

The Xoanon of Armed Aphrodite

The primary cult idol was an ancient xoanon — a rough wooden image of Aphrodite Urania (The Heavenly).

The love goddess on Cythera was depicted in full hoplite panoply — crested with a helmet, bearing a shield and a spear. This was a martial sovereign (Aphrodite Areia), guarding sea frontiers and granting victory in battle. For archaic Greeks, love and warfare were understood as two aspects of an irresistible elemental power.

The Sentry of Laconia

Beyond religious prestige, Cythera held strategic naval importance. The island overlooks the entrance to the Laconian Gulf, lying only miles off the Spartan coast and Cape Malea.

Sparta subdued Cythera in the 6th century BCE, garrisoning it and appointing a resident magistrate — the kytherodikes ("judge of Cythera"). Spartan leaders feared losing control of this threshold.

The Spartan sage Chilon (one of the Seven Sages), recognizing its vulnerability, pronounced:
"It were better for Cythera to sink beneath the sea than remain standing before the eyes of Laconia."

Chilon’s warning was confirmed during the Peloponnesian War (424 BCE):

  • The Athenian commander Nicias landed a fleet of 60 ships and seized Cythera;
  • The Athenians fortified the island, launching raids against the Spartan coastline and intercepting merchant convoys from Egypt;
  • For Sparta, the enemy presence on Aphrodite’s island was a severe strategic setback.

The Myth of the "Island of Love"

In post-classical culture, this rocky island became an allegory of idyllic romance.

In Renaissance and Enlightenment verse, Cythera (Cythera) became synonymous with a terrestrial paradise of sensual freedom. This motif culminated in French Rococo painting — notably Jean-Antoine Watteau’s "Pilgrimage to Cythera" (1717), where aristocratic couples set sail for an enchanted realm detached from ancient Phoenician dye-vats and Spartan garrisons.

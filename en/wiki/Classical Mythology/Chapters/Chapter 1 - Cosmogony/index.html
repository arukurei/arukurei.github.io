The Beginning

In the very beginning, there was nothing but Chaos — an endless, silent, and dark abyss...
It sounds rather gloomy already, though in my view, it was Hesiod's attempt to make the narrative more epic. For out of it — or rather simultaneously with it, as if by the will of some grand design—emerged the primordial entities that became the pillars of the cosmos:

  • Gaia — the bedrock of the earth, ready to receive incoming life (the material foundation of the world)
  • In its deepest depths yawned Tartarus — a desolate, sunless edge of the universe (the deepest place in the bowels of the Earth)
  • Eros — the great force of attraction and creation (Love/Creation)
Important

Without them, Chaos is unreal, for the primordial fundamentals cannot exist apart, and only together do they find meaning:

  • Eros is born of Chaos, but this is merely a quirk of sequential storytelling; in truth, they arise simultaneously as polarities.
  • Gaia and Tartarus represent matter and its absence, without which Chaos and Eros are devoid of meaning (if there is no matter, what is nothingness, and what does creation even signify?)

From the depths of Chaos are born Erebus and Nyx — Darkness and Night—particular manifestations of their progenitor, becoming its tangible embodiments within the world.

From the union of Nyx and Erebus arose that which promised transformation to such a dark existence — their opposites — Aether and Hemera, the luminous upper Air and bright Day.

Nyx also brings forth autonomously a vast multitude of entities (daimons) — predominantly grim, underscoring her stature as the wellspring of all future tribulations and constraints of mortal existence:

Thus, the universe took its first shape.

Metamorphosis

While the forces of the Protogenoi (the primal, primordial deities) remained mere incorporeal qualities and states of space, the world awaited its time.
The driving force of change becomes Eros — the instrument of the coming metamorphosis. Under his invisible influence, Gaia — the living, burning flesh of the young earth — creates the first chthonic deities:

  • Uranus — "an equal to herself, the Starry Sky, to cover her on every side, and to be an ever-sure abiding place for the blessed gods"
  • Following him, she brought forth the boundless, tempestuous primordial sea, Pontus, and Thalassa
  • Oros — the mountain ranges towering up toward the heavens

Uranus stretched out over Gaia, becoming her consort and the first ruler of the Universe. Every night he descended upon the Earth, and from their union, children were born.

How does the Sky emerge from nothing?

Mythological thinking does not ask this question. To myth, naming equals creating. The Earth becomes aware of itself as the "lower"—and thereby brings forth the "upper."

One can imagine this differently, no less faithful to the myth:

  • Physically: it is akin to the young dry land exhaling vapors and gases that condense into the celestial dome. This is not creation ex nihilo—Gaia divided herself, sending forth a part of her being into the heights.

  • The Sky is the boundary of the earth, its horizon and limit: As soon as something bounded exists (the Earth), that which bounds it (the Sky) must also exist. One inevitably defines the other.

Uranus and the First Union

Uranus became the first ruler of the cosmos and the consort of Gaia. This was the great union of Sky and Earth—a mother and her own offspring, two colossal primal forces locked in an eternal embrace.

The first creations of this union were beings of monstrous, primeval power. First into the world came three one-eyed giants — the First Cyclopes. In their wake were born three fifty-headed and hundred-handed behemoths — the Hecatoncheires, whose destructive might knew no bounds.

Uranus was appalled by the sight of his own brood. Fearing their savage power, he forcibly crammed the newborn giants back into the dark depths of the Earth—into bottomless Tartarus, inflicting agonizing, tearing pain upon Gaia.

Children of the Earth as Her Elements

Gaia is the raging, fierce, and blazing power of matter. Her first children are not merely monsters, but chthonic manifestations of the Earth itself, embodying subterranean fire, earthquakes, and tempests. By locking them in Tartarus, Uranus sought to suppress and tame the primal terrestrial elements themselves.

Despite her agony, Gaia brought forth the next generation of deities—the fair and majestic Titans, six brothers and six sisters:

  • Oceanus — the great freshwater river encircling the bounds of the inhabited world.
  • Tethys — the Titaness of moisture, nourishing the earth’s rivers and springs with her waters.
  • Coeus — the embodiment of inquiring intellect and the celestial axis.
  • Mnemosyne — memory itself, the future mother of the nine Muses.
  • Hyperion — the Titan of clear celestial light.
  • Theia — the Titaness of sight, who bestowed upon gold and precious stones their alluring luster; mother of Helios (the Sun), Selene (the Moon), and Eos (the Dawn).
  • Crius — the lord of constellations, ordering the movements of the night sky.
  • Phoebe — the Titaness of moonlit radiance and prophecy.
  • Themis — the steadfast embodiment of divine law, custom, and order.
  • Iapetus — the Titan of mortality and the fleeting nature of life (father of the Titan Prometheus).
  • Cronus — the youngest of the brothers, the most treacherous, ambitious, and cunning.
  • Rhea — the future consort of Cronus and the great mother of the Olympian gods.

They were radiant and full of majesty, yet Uranus’s fear of his own children did not wane. No sooner were the Titans born than their father hid them away within the womb of Gaia, forbidding them to step into the light and claim their place in the world.

Suffocating beneath the intolerable weight of the children trapped within her, consumed by pain and wrath, Gaia resolved to overthrow her ruthless consort...

The Rebellion of Cronus

In the deepest recesses of her depths, Gaia fashioned the Adamantine Sickle from a rare, unbreakable metal — the first weapon in the history of the universe. With it, she appealed to her Titan children, calling upon them to rise against the cruelty of their father.

An overwhelming dread of Heaven's majesty gripped all the Titans. Only the youngest, the cunning Cronus, dared to fulfill his mother's will.

When night fell, and Uranus, driven by lust, descended upon Gaia once more, Cronus struck the fatal blow. With the razor-sharp sickle, he castrated his father, stripping him forever of his virility. The blood of the wounded sky spilled upon the face of the Earth, and from these furious drops, Gaia brought forth the warlike Archaic Giants, the dread goddesses of vengeance — the Erinyes (Furies), and the Meliae — ash-tree nymphs, from whose wood humans would later fashion the first spears.

The severed flesh of Uranus fell into the churning sea. Around it foamed a pure white froth, from which, off the shores of sacred Cythera, the goddess of love, Aphrodite, was born.

The Two Aphrodites

The birth of Aphrodite from the foam and blood of the castrated Uranus is Hesiod's archaic version (Aphrodite Urania, born without a mother). In the later Homeric tradition, she appears in a gentler guise — as the daughter of the thunderer Zeus and the Oceanid Dione.

Having overthrown his father, Cronus proclaimed himself the new supreme ruler. He freed his Titan brothers and sisters, but left the hundred-handed giants and the Cyclopes to languish in the dark abyss of Tartarus, fearful of their insurmountable might.

The Evolution of Tyranny

Uranus was not a villain in the conventional sense. His cruelty sprang from a visceral terror of an uncontrollable future. He desperately sought to preserve the primordial, established, and familiar stasis of the world, wherein any transformation or novel creation inexorably disrupted that order. The dread of surrendering dominion and control eclipsed paternal affection, yet halting the evolution of existence proved impossible: it was precisely his frantic bid to obstruct the natural unfolding of the cosmos that precipitated the sovereign's fatal downfall.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, in his Bibliotheca, adds a crucial nuance to this myth: during the rebellion, Cronus temporarily freed the Hundred-Handed Ones and the Cyclopes to harness their strength against their father. But no sooner had victory been won than the new master bound them once more and cast them back into the abyss.

Here, the mythological motif of imprisonment shifts its fundamental nature. Uranus hid the children out of visceral, instinctive dread. Cronus, however, acts out of cold political calculation. This is the mindset of a tyrant eliminating an unruly force that might one day threaten his own throne.

The elder gods know no death — Uranus merely took his rightful place forever across the unreachable celestial vault: stripped of his former sway, he retreated into the heights, reduced to a silent celestial dome devoid of will and dominion. Yet before his departure, he uttered a fateful prophecy, shared by Gaia herself:

Cronus would suffer the very same fate, overthrown by his own son, setting in motion a merciless cycle of struggle for power.

This curse poisoned the mind of the new sovereign forever, planting within him an eternal fear of what was to come...

Аlkrei

A momentous event has transpired, one that will usher in an eternal struggle for the throne of Olympus. In my view, it is reckless to assume that even Zeus, as the world's ultimate sovereign, can maintain his grip upon it — given the fundamental nature of this ephemeral entropy.

The notion of a certain "doom of dominion" is alluring and practically begs for continuation. Within Aerythea, it becomes the bedrock of the Theomachy — a war of the gods in which the world will once again transform its established face, and Zeus will at last be overthrown.

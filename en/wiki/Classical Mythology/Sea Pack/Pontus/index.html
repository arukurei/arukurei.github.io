Pontus

Pontus (Greek Pontos — "sea") — the primordial, tempestuous, and boundless salt sea emerging at the earliest dawn of creation. In the cosmogony of Hesiod, Pontus is one of the earliest Protogenoi, representing the ensouled aquatic element of the Earth itself, harboring the unfathomable depths of the cosmos.

Parthenogenesis of Gaia

Gaia brings forth Pontus alone (parthenogenetically), without male union, even prior to her marriage to Uranus. If Uranus embodies celestial Height, then Pontus is the liquid flesh of the Earth, flowing forth from her depths to fill the profoundest chasms of the universe.

Pontus and Oceanus

In Greek mythology, two great primordial water deities coexist, their fundamental natures starkly contrasting:

  • The Titan Oceanus: the colossal freshwater river that encircles the land along the outermost rim of the world. Born from the union of Sky and Earth, he possesses conscious will and serves as the progenitor of all river deities.
  • Pontus (Protogenos): the internal salt sea — turbulent, pitch-dark, and treacherous to seafarers. Older than the Titans, he personifies the untamed, primal aquatic element.
The Legacy of Mother Earth

The profound affinity between Pontus and Oceanus is only natural: both trace their origin to Gaia. It was the Great Mother, as the foundation of all terrestrial matter, who imbued her sons with this fierce watery essence, making their ever-shifting tides an extension of her own elemental nature.

Pontus as the Progenitor of Sea Monsters

Uniting in union with Gaia, Pontus sired the first dwellers of the marine abyss, each embodying a distinct aspect of the sea’s fickle nature:

  1. Nereus: the "Old Man of the Sea" — a wise, truthful, and benevolent soothsayer, father of the lovely Nereids. He personifies the calm, crystalline, and gentle sea
  2. Thaumas: the god of marine wonders and ominous portents, father of the celestial messenger Iris the Rainbow and the predatory, storm-bearing Harpies
  3. Phorcys: the grim guardian of the ocean depths, embodying submerged reefs, shoals, and the hidden perils of the abyss
  4. Ceto: the terrifying goddess of the deep, mother to a monstrous brood and the personification of the sea's mortal hazards
  5. Eurybia: "The Wide-Strong," goddess of the untamable force of gale-force winds and subterranean ocean currents.
The Wellspring of Chthonic Dread

The sea’s first offspring were ancient sages and horrors.
To the ancient Hellenes, the salt sea was the most volatile and alien element, masking beneath its glassy azure expanse sudden ruin and primordial mysteries. Thus, Phorcys and Ceto became the wellspring of countless terrors of the Greek world: from their union sprang Medusa the Gorgon, the Graeae, Echidna, Scylla, and the hundred-headed dragon Ladon.

The Enigmatic Telchines: Sorcerers and Smiths of Rhodes

Beyond the five ancient sea deities, Pontus, in union with the sea goddess Thalassa, sired the Telchines — an enigmatic race of submarine sorcerers, metallurgists, and demigods who inhabited the island of Rhodes.

In ancient lore, the Telchines were depicted as eerie hybrids: human bodies with the heads of dogs and flipper-like, webbed hands. They wielded terrifying elemental sorcery: summoning torrential rains, hail, and snowstorms, shifting their shapes at will, and blighting all living things with their venomous gaze (the evil eye).

The Telchines entered myth as the first master metallurgists, having discovered the secrets of casting bronze and forging iron, and fashioning the earliest sacred effigies of the gods. Their zoomorphic appearance traces back to the archaic motif of the Dog-Headed Men (Cynocephali) — the most ancient prototype of canine-headed beings in global folklore.

Alternative Tradition: The Rhodian Variant

In the later texts of Hellenistic poets and chroniclers (Bacchylides, Callimachus, and Diodorus Siculus), the Telchines were credited with rearing the youthful Poseidon, forging his trident, and fashioning the Adamantine Sickle.

Info
  1. Bacchylides (5th century BCE) — the great Greek lyric poet and primary rival of Pindar. He composed celebratory hymns and odes honoring the victors of the Olympic Games. To flatter island patrons, Bacchylides wove their rare, regional folk traditions into his verses.
  2. Callimachus (3rd century BCE) — the premier poet and chief librarian of the Library of Alexandria! Callimachus was a consummate "myth hunter," thoroughly immersed in the scrolls of the Alexandrian archives, ferreting out the most obscure tales from the Greek islands and embedding them into his poetry.
  3. Diodorus Siculus (1st century BCE) — a Greek historian and author of the monumental 40-volume Bibliotheca Historica. He traveled extensively across the Mediterranean, setting down in detail the legends of Rhodes (including the saga of the Telchines in Book V).

Pontus in Later Lore

With the passage of time, Pontus recedes from the mythological stage as an active persona, dissolving into the very element he embodied. Later, sovereignty over the waters passed first to his son Nereus, and ultimately to the Olympian sovereign, Poseidon.

Yet primordial Pontus endured as the physical sea beneath the keels of Greek galleys. Memory of his name survived across the geography of the ancient world — echoed, most famously, in the Pontus Euxinus ("The Hospitable Sea"), known today as the Black Sea.

The Beginning

In the very beginning, nothing existed except Chaos — an infinite, silent, and tenebrous abyss...
This framing sounds bleak, yet it reflects Hesiod’s intention to endow the narrative with epic scale. For alongside Chaos — or rather, concurrently with it, as if ordered by a single grand design — the primordial entities that formed the bedrock of creation emerged:

  • Gaia — the foundation of the earth, prepared to sustain life (the material basis of the universe);
  • In her deepest recesses opened Tartarus — the dark, lightless limit of the cosmos (the deepest abyss within Earth);
  • Eros — the universal impulse of attraction and creation (Love/Generation).
Important

Without these entities Chaos cannot be conceived, for primeval principles cannot subsist in isolation; they derive meaning through one another:
- Eros emerges alongside Chaos, appearing concurrently as an ontological polarity;
- Gaia and Tartarus represent matter and its negative void, without which Chaos and Eros lack operational reality (if matter does not exist, what is non-being, and what does creation signify?).

From the depths of Chaos issued Erebus and Nyx — Gloom and Night, manifestations of their parent, operating as its tangible forms in space and time.

From the union of Nyx and Erebus emerged that which promised cosmic transfiguration — their radiant opposites — Aether and Hemera, luminous celestial Air and radiant Day.

Nyx also parthenogenetically engenders a host of entities (daemons) — predominantly ominous, emphasizing her standing as the fountainhead of mankind’s existential hardships:

Thus the cosmos assumed its first intelligible boundaries.

Metamorphosis

While the Protogenoi (the primeval deities) remained incorporeal qualities and spatial conditions, the cosmos awaited transformation.
The catalyst of this shift was Eros — the engine of impending metamorphosis.
Under his invisible influence, Gaia — the generative substance of the young earth — generated the first chthonic powers:

  • Uranus — "the starry Heaven equal to herself, to cover her on every side, and to be an ever-sure abiding-place for the blessed gods";
  • Following him, she brought forth the boundless salt expanse of Pontus and Thalassa;
  • Oros — the soaring mountain ranges reaching toward heaven.

Uranus spread across Gaia, becoming her consort and the first sovereign of the Cosmos. Nightly he descended upon the Earth, and from their union children were born.

How Does Heaven Emerge from Nothing?

Mythological consciousness does not pose this question. To it, naming equals generation. The Earth conceives of herself as "the lower" — and thereby brings forth "the upper".

This can be understood in two ways:

  • Physically: it mirrors the young land exhaling mists and vapors that condense into the sky. It is not creation ex nihilo — Gaia differentiated her own essence, releasing part of herself upward.

  • The Sky is the boundary of the earth, its horizon and limit. Once a bounded entity (Earth) exists, that which bounds it (Sky) is established. The one necessarily determines the other.

Uranus and the First Union

Uranus became the first ruler of the universe and the consort of Gaia. This was the grand union of Sky and Earth — mother and offspring, two primordial forces locked in continuous embrace.

The first children of this union were beings of terrifying primeval strength. First were born three one-eyed giants — the Elder Cyclopes. Following them came three fifty-headed and hundred-handed colossi — the Hecatoncheires, whose destructive power knew no bounds.

Uranus recoiled at the appearance of his offspring. Dreaded by their violent strength, he forced the newborn giants back into the dark womb of the Earth — into bottomless Tartarus, subjecting Gaia to agonizing pain.

The Children of Earth as Her Elements

Gaia represents the volcanic, churning substance of matter. Her first offspring were chthonic manifestations of the Earth herself, personifying subterranean fire, earthquakes, and tempests. By locking them within Tartarus, Uranus sought to suppress telluric forces.

Despite her torment, Gaia brought forth the next generation of gods — the magnificent Titans, six brothers and six sisters:

  • Oceanus — the freshwater river encircling the edges of the inhabited world;
  • Tethys — Titaness of moisture, feeding terrestrial rivers and springs;
  • Coeus — embodiment of the celestial axis and intellect;
  • Mnemosyne — memory personified, future mother of the nine Muses;
  • Hyperion — Titan of celestial light;
  • Theia — Titaness of sight, granting gold and gems their radiance; mother of Helios (Sun), Selene (Moon), and Eos (Dawn);
  • Crius — lord of constellations, ordering the nocturnal sky;
  • Phoebe — Titaness of lunar radiance and prophecy;
  • Themis — embodiment of divine law, custom, and cosmic order;
  • Iapetus — Titan of mortality and lifespan (father of Prometheus);
  • Cronus — youngest of the brothers, the most cunning and ambitious;
  • Rhea — future consort of Cronus and mother of the Olympian gods.

They were majestic, yet Uranus’s fear of his progeny did not subside. As each Titan was born, the father hid them within the earth, forbidding them from emerging into the light.

Tormented by the weight of her children buried within her, consumed by pain and wrath, Gaia resolved to overthrow her consort...

The Rebellion of Cronus

Within the depths of her being, Gaia fashioned the Adamantine Sickle from indestructible ore — the first weapon in cosmic history. Armed with it, she approached her Titan children, inciting them to rise against their father.

Terror of the Sky gripped the Titans. Only the youngest, cunning Cronus, resolved to execute his mother’s purpose.

When night fell and Uranus descended upon Gaia, Cronus struck. With the jagged sickle he castrated his father, severing his generative power. The blood of the wounded sky fell upon Earth, and from those drops Gaia brought forth the warlike Archaic Giants, the goddesses of retribution — the Erinyes (Furies), and the Meliae — nymphs of the ash tree, from whose wood spears would later be crafted.

The severed flesh of Uranus fell into the sea. Around it white foam formed, from which, near the shores of sacred Cythera, the goddess of love Aphrodite was born.

The Two Aphrodites

The emergence of Aphrodite from the foam and blood of mutilated Uranus is Hesiod’s archaic account (Aphrodite Urania, born without a mother). In Homer’s later tradition, she appears in an anthropomorphic domestic role — as the daughter of Zeus and Dione.

Having overthrown his father, Cronus proclaimed himself supreme ruler. He released his Titan siblings, yet left the Hecatoncheires and Cyclopes bound within Tartarus, dreading their strength.

The Evolution of Tyranny

Uranus was not a villain in the conventional sense. His cruelty stemmed from an existential dread of an uncontrollable future. He desperately sought to preserve the static, primordial stability of the universe, whereas any change and new creation inevitably shattered that stasis. Fear of losing control overpowered paternal duty, yet arresting the evolution of being proved impossible: it was his attempt to freeze cosmic motion that directly caused his downfall.

Pseudo-Apollodorus notes in his "Bibliotheca" that during the revolt Cronus temporarily freed the Hundred-Handed Ones and Cyclopes to secure victory. Yet once the throne was secured, the new sovereign bound them again and cast them back into the abyss.

The motif of imprisonment shifts its character here. Uranus acted out of primal horror; Cronus acted out of political calculation. This marks the mindset of a tyrant eliminating potential rivals to his throne.

The ancient gods cannot die — Uranus took his place in the celestial heights: stripped of authority, he withdrew into an unmoving starry canopy.
Yet before departing, he uttered a prophecy:
Cronus would suffer the same fate, overthrown by his own son, perpetuating the cycle of struggle for cosmic dominion.

This curse haunted the new sovereign, lodging an enduring dread of the future in his mind...

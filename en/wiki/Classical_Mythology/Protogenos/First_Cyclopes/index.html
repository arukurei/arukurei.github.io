First Cyclopes

The First Cyclopes (Greek Κύκλωπες, Kyklopes — "Round-Eyed") — three primordial Uranian giants born of the union between Gaia and Uranus. Contrary to later folklore depicting wild man-eating behemoths, the First Cyclopes are ancient divine demiurges personifying the incandescent fury of lightning and storm, the arcana of subterranean fire, and the sacred craft of smithing.

It was they who forged the supreme armaments of the Olympian sovereigns, determining the outcome of the ten-year cosmic war fought by the Olympians against the Titans.

Names and the Elemental Triad

In the canonical theogony of Hesiod, the First Cyclopes emerge not as an anonymous horde, but as a sacred triad of brothers. The name of each reveals a distinct phase of celestial lightning discharge:

  • Brontes (Βρόντης) — "The Thunderer" (the personification of deafening, rolling thunder that shakes the bedrock)
  • Steropes (Στερόπης) — "The Lightning-Flash" (the embodiment of zigzagging lightning cleaving the celestial vault)
  • Arges (Ἄργης) — "The Bright" or "The White-Shining" (the blinding flash of light, the incinerating heat of the thunderstrike)
Hesiod on the Nature of the First Cyclopes

And again, three other sons were born of Earth and Heaven,
Like unto the gods in all things else,
But a single eye was set in the midst of their foreheads:
One circular eye alone was set in their brow.
And they were surnamed Cyclopes (Round-Eyed) because
One solitary orb-shaped eye gleamed in the center.
Strength and might and craft were in all their deeds.
— Hesiod, Theogony

Mythological Anthropology: The Metallurgical Eye

The solitary round eye in the center of the Cyclopes' foreheads is no mere grotesque fantasy. Historians of religion and archaeologists link this image to the practical reality of bronze-casters in the Mycenaean and pre-Mycenaean eras:

  1. Ancient metalsmiths often wore a protective leather patch over one eye to shield their sight from flying embers and molten slag;
  2. Scholars perceive in the Cyclops's circular eye a metaphor for the round orifice of the smelting furnace (crucible) from which roaring fire blazes;
  3. An additional catalyst for the myth were discoveries in Mediterranean karst caverns of fossilized dwarf elephant skulls: the central nasal cavity for the trunk could easily have been mistaken by ancient Greeks for a solitary central eye socket.

Three Generations of Cyclopes: A Crucial Distinction

In late classical lore and modern culture, disparate orders of cyclopes are frequently conflated, despite possessing radically different origins and symbolic weight.

Three Distinct Natures of Cyclopes
  1. The First Cyclopes (Uranians): sons of Uranus and Gaia — Brontes, Steropes, and Arges. Divine craftsmen, smiths of lightning, and immortal allies of Zeus.
  2. Homeric Cyclopes (Pastoral Giants): figures from Homer's Odyssey (including Polyphemus). A feral race of one-eyed shepherds dwelling in caves on a remote isle (traditionally Sicily). They are the offspring of Poseidon, knowing neither civic laws, tilled agriculture, nor artisan crafts; they spurn the Olympian gods and devour strangers.
  3. The Builder Cyclopes (Lycian Cyclopes): a legendary clan of primeval stonemasons from Asia Minor, hired by Mycenaean kings Proetus and Perseus to erect the colossal fortifications of Tiryns and Mycenae.

Death of the Demiurges: The Vengeance of Apollo

Despite their divine majesty, the First Cyclopes were swept into a tragic generational strife within the Olympian family.

When the son of Apollo, the divine physician Asclepius, attained such mastery that he began raising the dead from the dust — breaching a fundamental law of the cosmos — Zeus blasted him with a thunderbolt. Daring not raise his hand against the supreme Thunderer himself, grief-stricken Apollo turned his wrath upon the makers of the fatal weapon:

  • Apollo marched into the forges of the Cyclopes and slew them with his unerring golden arrows (according to Euripides in the tragedy Alcestis);
  • In punishment for slaying the artisans of the sacred thunderbolts, Zeus was on the verge of casting Apollo into Tartarus, but softened the penalty upon the entreaties of his mother, Leto: Apollo was banished from Olympus for a year and reduced to serving the mortal king Admetus as a thrall, meekly tending his flocks.

The Subterranean Hammers of Hephaestus (The Hellenistic Canon)

In later Hellenistic and Roman traditions (in Callimachus and Virgil's Aeneid), the myth of the slain Cyclopes was reinterpreted: they did not vanish, but transitioned into immortal assistants to the god of smithing and fire, Hephaestus.

Their subterranean forge was set within the fiery belly of Mount Etna in Sicily and the Aeolian (Lipari) Islands. The ancients believed that the rhythmic thunder, billowing ash, and seismic tremors surrounding Sicilian volcanoes were the handiwork of Brontes, Steropes, and their companion Pyracmon ("The Fiery Anvil"), tirelessly forging new thunderbolts for the master of Olympus.

"Cyclopean Masonry": An Imprint on Architecture

The memory of the cyclopes left an indelible mark on the tangible material culture of Hellas. When Greeks of the classical era came upon the megalithic ruins of 2nd-millennium BCE Mycenaean citadels (the walls of Mycenae, Argos, and Tiryns), they refused to believe such fortifications could have been reared by mere mortals.

Erected without mortar from rough-hewn limestone monoliths weighing tens of tons (up to 12–15 tons per block), these structures came to be known as "Cyclopean masonry." To the Hellenic mind, only the titanic might of the earliest primeval children of Uranus and Gaia could heave such boulders and raise the unassailable Lion Gate.
